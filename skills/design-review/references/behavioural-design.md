# Behavioural design

Use these principles to diagnose a concrete obstacle, then propose a change and an observable check. A named effect is a hypothesis about this experience, not proof that a tactic will improve it.

## Honest progress: goal-gradient effect

When a task has a meaningful endpoint, make the remaining work understandable: for example, "Step 2 of 4". Use percentages or time estimates only when they reflect actual work. Account for branching paths rather than promising a finish that keeps moving. Keep setup completion distinct from demonstrated learning or another useful outcome. Streaks and trackers are optional; do not add them to every experience.

## Meaningful success and endings: peak-end rule

Make a real achievement legible and finish cleanly: explain what happened, what was saved and what the user can do next. The end means the final part of the experience, not necessarily a negative moment. In learning, acknowledge what someone demonstrated without claiming mastery from completing a screen. Celebration should fit the achievement; confetti and praise are not defaults. Repair errors and frustrating interactions rather than masking them with a success screen.

## Transparent processing: labor illusion

Show useful information about work that is actually happening during a necessary wait. Keep available results fast. Do not add artificial delays, fabricated checks or fake progress to suggest greater care or security. The research below concerns perceived service value in particular settings; it does not establish that a slower verification process is safer or universally more trustworthy.

## Restrained distinctiveness: Von Restorff effect

Give the primary action or consequential information a clear visual priority. Start with typography, contrast, placement and proximity; use an icon or illustration when it communicates something useful. Consistent plain lists often improve comparison. Making every item distinctive defeats prioritisation and creates noise.

## Manageable decisions: choice overload

Judge choices by their relevance, complexity and the user's familiarity, not a universal maximum count. Group related options, explain important differences and provide reversible defaults when appropriate. Use search or filters when the collection warrants them. Do not hide useful choices or silently choose a user's interests merely to reduce the option count.

## Source and adoption, 30 October 2026

These judgments were agreed after discussing a user-supplied summary of "The psychology trick that makes any app feel 10x better". The original video URL and underlying benchmark data were not supplied; the video itself was not reviewed. Its "10x" framing and claim that progress bars increase onboarding completion are unverified and must not become forecasts or universal rules.

Relevant primary research reviewed during the discussion:

- [Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), goal-gradient research](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2733214): reward-program findings provide context, not direct evidence that every onboarding progress bar helps.
- [Buell and Norton (2011), The Labor Illusion](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf): experiments simulated travel and dating services. We adopted transparent real processing and rejected blanket advice to manufacture delays.

The remaining guidance is context-dependent design judgment adopted from the discussion, not a verified conversion claim. Check task completion, errors, comprehension or retained use as relevant; a completion-rate increase alone does not establish a better experience.
